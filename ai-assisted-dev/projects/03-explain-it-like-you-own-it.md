# Project 3: Explain It Like You Own It

## Description

An interview drill, run for real. Take a piece of AI-assisted code from your own work — ideally from Project 1's audit or Project 2's reviewed project, but any real AI-assisted code you can genuinely stand behind works — and prepare a walkthrough of it exactly as if an interviewer had just said "walk me through this." Cover what it does, why this approach specifically, its tradeoffs and limits, and what you changed after verifying it. Deliver the walkthrough (recorded video/audio, or a tightly written transcript-style document) as though a real interviewer is listening, using the structure Chapter 6 lays out. This project produces both practice and a reusable artifact — the same story, ready to tell for real.

## Difficulty & Estimated Effort

**Intermediate.** Estimated effort: 2–3 hours.

## Chapters Used

- Chapter 2 — The Verification Workflow (what you're narrating actually happened)
- Chapter 6 — Interviews in the AI Era (the structure and behavioral-question prep)

## Requirements

- [ ] Choose one piece of your own AI-assisted code — a function, a small module, or one meaningful piece of a larger project — that you can explain in 3–7 minutes.
- [ ] Write brief prep notes (not a script to read verbatim) covering the four required parts: what it does, why this approach over alternatives, its tradeoffs/limits, and what you changed after verifying it — the last part must reference a real, specific thing you caught, not a generic claim.
- [ ] Deliver the walkthrough as if speaking to an interviewer: either record yourself (audio or video, a phone recording is fine) or write it as a tight, spoken-style transcript — not bullet points, actual sentences you'd say out loud.
- [ ] Separately, prepare and answer, in writing, one behavioral question from Chapter 6's list (e.g., "tell me about a time AI gave you wrong or misleading code — how did you catch it?"), using a real, specific example — reuse Project 1's flaw if it fits.
- [ ] Prepare and answer, in writing, a second behavioral question of your own choosing from the same family (workflow, prompting decisions, or when you chose not to use AI).
- [ ] Self-review your recording or transcript against this checklist: Did you ever say or imply "the AI just did that" without following it with your own verification story? Did you name a specific tradeoff instead of a vague one ("faster" is vague; "trades a small memory overhead for O(n) instead of O(n²)" is specific)?
- [ ] Revise once based on that self-review.

## Hints

- If you're recording, don't aim for a polished single take — do two or three passes and pick the best; the goal is fluency under mild pressure, not a produced video.
- The "why this approach" part is usually the weakest in a first pass. If you catch yourself saying "because that's what the AI suggested," push further: why did that suggestion make sense, or what would you change now that you understand it better?
- Reusing Project 1's audited module is efficient — you already have a documented, real flaw and fix, which is exactly the strongest kind of material for the "what did you change after verifying" part.
- Time yourself. Interview answers that run long lose the interviewer; practicing the 3–7 minute constraint now is part of the skill.

## Stretch Goals

- Do the same drill live with a peer, mentor, or study-group partner playing interviewer, including unscripted follow-up questions.
- Prepare a second walkthrough for a piece of code you wrote entirely without AI, and compare which one was easier to explain fluently — and why.
- Turn your two written behavioral-question answers into a small personal "interview story bank" file you keep adding to as you build more projects.
